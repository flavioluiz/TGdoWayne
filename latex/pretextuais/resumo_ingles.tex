This dissertation investigates how frequency compression and likelihood approximations affect graviton-mass inference with pulsar timing arrays. Linear massive-gravitational-wave propagation provides the basis for paired experiments using complex Fourier coefficients, quadratic estimators, and Gaussian controls with matching moments.

Failure of the normal approximation for the physical estimators is a central result: in the tensor campaign of 500 realizations, ten calibration rejections remained under the assessed numerical uncertainty. In the correctly specified Gaussian control, compression reduced mean information about mass by $0.06211\pm0.00477$ and $0.10631\pm0.00695$ nat in two configurations using catalog sky positions; uncertainties are Monte Carlo standard errors of the mean. These results separate information loss from likelihood misspecification. Primary contrasts between dispersive and approximate responses remained inconclusive at a scale of 0.01 of the prior width.

Interpreting mass bounds also requires distinguishing information from prior support. A constant likelihood and a uniform prior on $u=f_gT\in[0,1]$ yield $Q_{0.95}(u)=0.95$ and zero posterior-to-prior divergence: the bound $m_gc^2\leq0.95h_{\mathrm P}/T$ is entirely prior determined. The Fierz--Pauli helicity-zero extension preserves the link between transverse and longitudinal deformations and identifies a degeneracy between scalar and tensor powers at the propagation threshold. Using synthetic signals, noise, and distances, the study establishes criteria for future observational analyses: assess likelihood calibration, quantify information relative to the prior, and evaluate response approximations with sufficient numerical precision.
